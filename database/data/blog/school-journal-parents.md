Every week, something remarkable comes home in your child's school bag and is almost always wasted: their marked class work. A test with a teacher's ticks and crosses, a comment in the margin, a score — this is among the most honest, trustworthy information you will ever get about what your child actually knows. And most families glance at the number, sign the page, and let it disappear. Learning to mine that marked work for insight is one of the cheapest, highest-value habits a SEA parent can build.

## Why marked school work is such good data

Home practice and apps are useful, but a child's marked school work has three qualities they struggle to match:

- **It is assessed by someone who knows your child** and the curriculum — the teacher, in context.
- **It happens under real conditions** — a timed, sit-still, no-help classroom test, closer to the SEA than a relaxed session at home.
- **It reflects what the school is emphasising now**, which is a clue to what is coming next.

A weakness that shows up on a real class test is one you can *trust*. That makes marked school work the ideal input for deciding what your child should work on — far better than guessing, or practising whatever a worksheet happens to cover.

## Reading past the score

The score at the top is the least useful thing on the page. The insight is in the *detail*:

**Which questions were wrong?** Sort them the way you would [past-paper errors](/blog/sea-past-papers): genuine content gaps, misreadings, careless slips, or unfinished-through-time. Each points to a different fix. Three careless slips is a checking-routine problem; three blank questions at the end is a pacing problem; three wrong on the same topic is a content gap to re-teach.

**What does the teacher's comment say — really?** Comments are often gentle. "Needs to show more working" may mean the child is guessing. "Careless errors" may mean rushing under pressure. Read them as diagnostic clues, not just encouragement or reproach.

**Is there a pattern across weeks?** One test is a snapshot; several tell a story. If ratio keeps appearing in the wrong column, that is a stable signal worth acting on. If a topic was weak in March and is fine now, that is progress worth noting — and worth *re-checking* later for [retention](/blog/measuring-progress-sea).

## From the page to the plan

Insight only matters if it changes what your child does. The loop is simple: the marked work reveals a specific gap → that gap becomes the focus of the next few practice sessions → the next class test checks whether it closed. This is precisely the [feedback loop](/blog/journaling-feedback-loop) that turns a marked test from a dead end into an engine. The class test stops being a verdict and becomes a diagnosis you act on.

Done consistently, this also gives you the cross-channel view that catches problems early. If your child aces the topic in relaxed home practice but keeps missing it on the timed school test, the issue is not knowledge — it is exam technique or nerves, which need a different response entirely. That comparison is only possible when the school work and the home practice sit in the same picture, the [consolidation](/blog/consolidating-learning-data) argument in action.

## Making it sustainable

The honest problem is that doing this by hand, every week, for every subject, is a lot. Photographing tests, sorting errors, spotting patterns across months, and adjusting practice accordingly is real work that busy parents rarely sustain past a few weeks of good intentions. The information is gold, but mining it manually is exhausting — which is why most families give up and the marked work goes back in the bag.

This is exactly the kind of bookkeeping worth handing to a tool: capture the marked work, read it, sort the errors, track the pattern over time, and fold it into the child's practice plan — so the insight actually gets used instead of admired once and forgotten. The parent still supplies judgement and knowledge of their own child; the tool supplies the tireless record-keeping. A word of caution applies, though: any reading of a photographed test can be imperfect, so treat each entry as one signal to reconcile with others, not an infallible verdict.

## What to do with this

- Stop treating marked school work as a **number to sign** — it is your best data.
- Read past the score: **which** questions were wrong, and **why**.
- Look for **patterns across weeks**, and feed each gap into the next practice.
- Use the school-vs-home comparison to tell **knowledge gaps from technique gaps**.
- Where the manual work is unsustainable, let a tool do the record-keeping — honestly.

The marked test in your child's bag right now is telling you exactly what to work on. The only question is whether you will read it, act on it, and check back — or let it join all the others in the bin.
