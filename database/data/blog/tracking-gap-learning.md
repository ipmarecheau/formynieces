Step back from the specifics of SEA preparation — the papers, the lessons, the hours — and a single, structural problem comes into view that underlies almost all the others. Your child's learning is happening in several places at once, and **no one is holding the whole picture.** Not the school, not the tutor, not the app, and usually not you. Each party sees a fragment; the complete child exists nowhere. This is the tracking gap, and it is the core problem this whole blog keeps circling back to.

## Where your child's learning actually lives

Think about all the places a SEA child is learning, and who can see each:

- **School** sees classwork, class tests, and the child's behaviour in a group — but only within school, and reports it to you a few times a year.
- **Extra lessons** see the child in another group, cover their own sequence, and rarely report specifics to you at all.
- **Apps and worksheets** at home generate scores, but each in its own silo, measuring its own slice.
- **Home** — you — sees homework, conversation, and a general vibe.
- **The child's own experience** — what they find hard, what they dread, what they secretly do not understand — lives only in their head, and children are not reliable reporters of their own gaps.

Every one of these holds a real, valuable fragment of the truth. And **none of them talks to the others.** The school does not know what the tutor is finding. The tutor does not see the app scores. The app does not know the child bombed a class test on the same topic. You get scattered pieces, at different times, in different formats, and are somehow expected to assemble them into a strategy.

## Why fragments don't add up

You might think that seeing several fragments is nearly as good as seeing the whole. It is not, for a specific reason: **the most important signals only appear when you combine the fragments.**

Consider: the school reports "satisfactory" in maths. The tutor says the child is "coming along." The app shows 70-something percent. Individually, each is reassuring. But combine them and a pattern might emerge that none could see alone — that *ratio* specifically is weak in all three, or that the child performs well in the low-pressure app and badly in the timed class test, revealing an anxiety problem rather than a knowledge one. The insight lives in the *combination*, and the combination is exactly what no one is doing.

This is why parents so often get blindsided on results day or at a mock: the warning signs were there, but scattered across sources that never met. We described the parent's-eye view of this in [Why Parents Can't See What Their Child Is Learning](/blog/parent-visibility-learning); the tracking gap is the structural cause behind it.

## The costs of the gap

The fragmentation is not a minor inconvenience. It produces concrete harm:

**Wasted effort.** Without a combined view, you cannot [target the real weakness](/blog/measuring-progress-sea), so effort scatters and children do [more hours](/blog/how-many-hours-sea-prep) than they need on the wrong things.

**Late discovery.** Problems that would be obvious in a combined view stay hidden until they surface in a high-stakes moment, too late to fix cheaply.

**Duplicated and contradictory work.** School, lessons and home may all drill the same mastered topic while a genuine gap goes untouched, because no one can see what the others are doing.

**No accountability.** You cannot tell which of your investments — which lesson, which app, which hour — is actually working, because their effects are never seen together.

## What closing the gap would mean

Closing the tracking gap does not require anything exotic. It requires one thing: **a single place that consolidates the signals from a child's different learning channels into one honest, current picture.** A place where practice results, school performance, and reading all feed into the same view of *this topic is solid, this one is slipping, work on this next* — regardless of which channel the signal came from.

That is the argument we make in full in [The Case for One Place for All Your Child's Learning Data](/blog/consolidating-learning-data), and it is why a platform that ingests a child's actual school work — not just its own exercises — matters. When the school's marked test can sit alongside the child's practice history in one view, the combined signal finally becomes visible. That is the whole idea behind treating a [school journal as a feedback loop](/blog/journaling-feedback-loop).

## An honest caveat

No single platform will ever capture *everything* about a child — a good teacher's intuition, a parent's knowledge of their own child, the things that never get written down. Consolidation is not about pretending one dashboard is the whole truth. It is about getting the *measurable* fragments into one place so the patterns between them stop being invisible. That is a large improvement over the status quo of total fragmentation, and it is honest about its limits.

## What to do with this

- Recognise that your child's learning is **fragmented** across school, lessons, apps and home.
- Understand that the most important signals appear only when fragments are **combined**.
- Treat the tracking gap as the **root cause** behind wasted effort and late-discovered problems.
- Look for a single place that **consolidates** the real signals into one honest, current picture.

The tracking gap is rarely named, but once you see it you cannot unsee it. Almost every frustration of SEA preparation — the wasted hours, the nasty surprises, the sense of flying blind — traces back to the same thing: nobody can see the whole child. Fixing that is the highest-leverage move a family can make.
