In Trinidad and Tobago, extra lessons are almost a rite of passage. For many families, signing a child up for after-school and weekend lessons is simply what you *do* when the SEA approaches — a marker of taking it seriously. Lessons can genuinely help, and many dedicated tutors do excellent work. But the standard group-lesson model has a structural blind spot that is worth understanding clearly, because it may explain why a child who attends faithfully still is not improving where it counts.

## The pace problem

A lesson with fifteen or twenty children moves at one pace — and that pace can only ever suit some of them. The tutor must pick a speed. Go slow enough for the strugglers and the strong children coast, bored, learning little that is new. Go fast enough for the strong children and the strugglers fall behind, quietly lost, nodding along to material that left them two steps back.

Your child is somewhere on that spread, and unless they happen to sit exactly at the pace the tutor picked, some of every lesson is either too fast or too slow for them specifically. This is the same limitation that afflicts [one-size-fits-all lessons](/blog/one-size-fits-all-lessons) everywhere: one pace cannot fit a room of different children.

## The diagnosis problem

Here is the deeper issue. In a large group, the tutor cannot possibly know the precise state of each child's understanding. They see who raises a hand, who looks confused, who does well on the occasional marked work. But they cannot know that *your* child understood fractions until last Tuesday and then quietly lost the thread on equivalent fractions, while appearing to keep up.

So the lesson covers the syllabus topic by topic, on schedule, whether or not each individual child has actually mastered the previous step. A child with an un-mastered foundation sits through lesson after lesson built on that shaky base, understanding less and less, while the register says they attended everything. The attendance is real; the learning is not.

## The visibility problem — for you

As a parent, what do you actually *see* from extra lessons? Usually: that your child went, that they have a book of notes, perhaps an occasional score. What you rarely see is a clear, honest, up-to-date picture of which topics your child has genuinely mastered and which are collapsing. You are paying for a service whose output you cannot really inspect.

That is not necessarily anyone's fault — a tutor with twenty children cannot produce a detailed diagnostic for each. But it means you are often making a significant financial and time commitment on faith, without the information you would need to know whether it is working. This is a concrete instance of the [parent visibility gap](/blog/parent-visibility-learning) that runs through the whole SEA system.

## When lessons *do* work

None of this means extra lessons are worthless — far from it. Lessons tend to work best when:

- The group is **small** enough that the tutor can actually track each child.
- The tutor gives **specific feedback** on your child's real errors, not just general instruction.
- There is genuine **two-way communication** with you about where your child stands.
- The lessons **complement** targeted home practice on your child's specific gaps, rather than substituting for it.

A great tutor who knows your child well is a wonderful thing. The problem is not tutoring; it is the *large-group, one-pace, low-visibility* version of it that many families default to because it is what is available and expected.

## The questions to ask

Before committing — or continuing — ask any lesson provider the questions in [Questions Every Parent Should Ask a Tutor](/blog/questions-to-ask-tutor). The most important ones are simple: *How will you know what my child specifically struggles with? How will you tell me? What happens when my child hasn't mastered something before the class moves on?* If there is no good answer, you are likely buying attendance, not learning.

## Filling the blind spot

The structural weakness of group lessons — one pace, weak diagnosis, low visibility — is precisely what personalised, adaptive practice is built to address. Not to replace a good tutor, but to make sure that between lessons, your child is working on *their* actual gaps, at *their* pace, and that *you* can see it. We discuss what that looks like in [What "Adaptive Learning" Really Means](/blog/adaptive-learning-explained).

## What to do with this

- Recognise that group lessons run at **one pace** that cannot fit every child.
- Understand that a tutor with many children **cannot diagnose** each one precisely.
- Ask what visibility *you* will get, and treat vague answers as a warning.
- Pair lessons with **targeted home practice** on your child's real gaps.

Extra lessons can be part of a strong SEA plan. But going, by itself, is not the same as learning — and the child who attends everything can still arrive at the exam with the same untouched weaknesses they started with, simply because no one ever saw them.
