Ask a room of SEA parents to define a "composite score" and you will get a dozen different answers. That confusion is understandable — the number is never printed on a homework sheet — but it matters, because the composite score is the number that decides your child's secondary school. This guide explains what it is, how cut-offs are set, and why last year's cut-off is a guide and not a promise.

## What a composite score actually is

Your child sits three papers: Mathematics, English Language Arts (ELA), and Creative Writing. Each raw mark is first **scaled**, then **weighted**, and finally added together. Per the Ministry of Education's [SEA Assessment Framework](https://storage.moe.gov.tt/wpdevelopment/2023/10/ASSESSMENT-FRAMEWORK-FOR-SEA-2025-2028.pdf), Mathematics carries the full weight, ELA around 60%, and Creative Writing around 40%. The single weighted total is the composite score.

Two children can have identical composites while being very different students — one strong in maths and weaker in writing, the other the reverse. Placement does not care which; it only reads the composite. That is why we always tell parents that [how placement works](/blog/how-sea-placement-works) rewards *balance* across the three weighted areas, not a spike in any one.

### Why scaling exists

Scaling adjusts for the fact that one year's paper may be harder than another's. Without it, a child who sat a brutal maths paper would be unfairly punished against a cohort that got an easier one. Scaling is the exam board's attempt to make scores comparable across years and papers. You cannot influence it — but knowing it exists explains why a "raw" mark your child mentions after the exam is not the number that counts.

## How cut-off scores are set

Here is the part that trips up most families: **there is no fixed pass mark.** A cut-off score is not decided in advance. It emerges from the placement process.

The system ranks every child by composite score, then works down the list, placing each child into the highest school on their choice list that still has space. When a popular school fills up, the composite score of the *last* child placed there becomes that school's cut-off for the year. Community trackers such as [SEAscores.NET](https://seascores.net/blog) collect these historical cut-offs so parents can see the pattern.

Because cut-offs are produced by the interaction of *this year's* scores and *this year's* choices, they move. A school that needed a composite of 90 last year might need 88 or 92 this year, depending on how the cohort performed and how many families chose it. Treat published cut-offs as a **range and a guide**, never a guaranteed threshold.

## Why "just aim for the cut-off" is bad advice

Parents sometimes reverse-engineer a target: "The school we want was 89 last year, so we need 89." The logic is tempting but risky, for three reasons.

1. **The target moves.** Aiming for last year's exact figure gives you no safety margin if this year's cut-off rises.
2. **The composite hides the mix.** An 89 built on a strong maths score is more robust than an 89 propped up by one exceptional writing paper, because maths carries more weight and is more consistently examinable.
3. **It encourages teaching to a number instead of to understanding.** Children who are coached to hit a mark, rather than to genuinely understand the material, tend to crumble on unfamiliar questions — exactly what a scaled, standardised exam is designed to include.

A healthier target is simply: *make each of the three weighted areas as strong as it can honestly be, with maths never the neglected one.* The composite takes care of itself.

## The measurement problem underneath all of this

Notice what every sensible strategy above requires: knowing, right now, how strong your child is in each of the three areas. Yet most families cannot answer that with any precision. A weekly lesson yields an overall impression. A school report arrives once a term. Neither breaks progress down by the weighted areas the composite actually rewards.

This is the real gap. You cannot manage a composite you cannot see the parts of. We explore this at length in [Measuring SEA Progress: Beyond the Weekly Test Score](/blog/measuring-progress-sea) and in the broader [tracking gap](/blog/tracking-gap-learning) — the fact that no single place shows you the whole child.

## What to do with this

- Stop treating the SEA as three separate subjects and start thinking in terms of one **weighted composite**.
- Look up several years of cut-offs for your target schools, and read them as a range.
- Give maths the respect its weighting demands — it is the heaviest single component.
- Insist on knowing where your child stands in each weighted area *this week*, not once a term.

The composite score can feel like a black box. It is not — it is just three weighted parts added together. Once you can see those parts clearly and honestly, SEA preparation stops being guesswork and becomes a matter of putting the next hour where it will move the number most.
