Imagine a shoe shop that sold only one size. Some customers would be lucky — it would fit. Most would leave with shoes too tight or too loose, and blame their own feet. This is, more or less, how a great deal of teaching works: one lesson, one pace, one explanation, delivered to a room of children whose needs are anything but uniform. The children for whom it fits do well. The rest are quietly left behind — and often conclude the problem is *them*.

## The middle-of-the-room problem

Any lesson delivered to a group has to choose a pace and a level. Rationally, teachers aim for the middle — fast enough not to bore the strong, slow enough not to lose the weak. But "the middle" is a compromise that fits no individual child exactly.

- The child who is **ahead** on this topic sits through an explanation they did not need, disengaging, learning to tune out.
- The child who is **behind** on this topic hears an explanation built on a foundation they do not yet have, and falls further back while nodding along.
- Only the child sitting near the middle, *on this particular topic today*, is genuinely well served.

And here is the crucial part: **no child is in the middle on every topic.** A student might be ahead on fractions, dead-centre on measurement, and badly behind on ratio. A single pace cannot serve that child across a syllabus, because their needs move topic to topic while the lesson's pace stays fixed.

## Why the strugglers suffer most

The child who is behind pays the highest price, because learning compounds. Miss the foundation of a topic and everything built on it becomes harder, until the child is not just behind but *lost* — sitting in lessons that assume knowledge they never secured. This is how a bright child ends up believing they are "bad at maths": not from lack of ability, but from a pace that never waited for them to master the last step before racing to the next.

This is the same mechanism we describe in [The Hidden Problem With Extra Lessons](/blog/problem-with-extra-lessons) — group lessons inherit exactly this flaw — and it is why [mastery learning](/blog/mastery-learning-reteach), which refuses to move on until a step is secure, is such a powerful corrective.

## Why the strong children also lose

It is tempting to think one-size-fits-all only hurts strugglers, and that strong children are fine. They are not. A capable child made to sit through material they have mastered learns something corrosive: that school is boring and their time is not valued. They coast, stop being challenged, and never develop the habit of effort — which catches up with them later, when the work finally gets hard and they have no experience of struggling productively.

Genuinely personalised learning stretches these children *forward*, giving them harder material the moment they are ready, instead of parking them at the class average.

## The honest defence of the classroom

To be fair, a single teacher with thirty children and a fixed syllabus and timetable cannot realistically deliver thirty different lessons at thirty different paces. One-size-fits-all is not laziness; it is what the constraints of a mass classroom force. Good teachers fight it heroically — with group work, differentiated worksheets, and extra attention where they can. But they are swimming against the structure.

The point is not to blame teachers. It is to recognise that the *fixed-pace group model itself* has a ceiling, and to ask what can be done outside it.

## What personalisation actually changes

The alternative is not one-on-one human tutoring for every child — that is unaffordable at scale. It is *adaptive* practice: material that adjusts its pace and difficulty to the individual child, waits for mastery before moving on, and re-teaches the specific thing a child missed rather than repeating the whole lesson. We explain how this works, and its limits, in [What "Adaptive Learning" Really Means](/blog/adaptive-learning-explained) and [Can AI Really Personalise a Child's Learning?](/blog/ai-personalised-learning).

Done honestly, this gives every child something the single-pace lesson cannot: work pitched at *their* level, on *their* gaps, right now. The strong child gets stretched; the struggling child gets the foundation rebuilt before the next brick is laid.

## What to do with this

- Recognise that any single-pace lesson fits only the **middle**, and no child is in the middle on every topic.
- Watch for a struggling child falling further behind, and a strong child quietly disengaging — both are symptoms of the same problem.
- Don't blame the child (or the teacher) for a structural limitation.
- Add **personalised, adaptive practice** so each child works at their own pace on their own gaps.

One size fits all is a compromise the mass classroom is forced into. Your child does not have to live entirely inside that compromise. The closer their practice gets to *their* actual level and *their* actual gaps, the less they are left behind — at either end of the room.
