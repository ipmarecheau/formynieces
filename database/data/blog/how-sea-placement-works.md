Every year around 18,000 children in Trinidad and Tobago sit the Secondary Entrance Assessment, and every year thousands of parents discover — often too late — that they never fully understood how the exam turns into a school. If you are preparing a child for the SEA, the single most useful thing you can do early is understand the machinery behind placement. It changes how you prioritise study time.

## One exam, three weighted parts

The SEA tests three areas: Mathematics, English Language Arts (ELA), and a Creative Writing paper. What surprises many parents is that these do not count equally. According to the Ministry of Education's [Assessment Framework for SEA 2025–2028](https://storage.moe.gov.tt/wpdevelopment/2023/10/ASSESSMENT-FRAMEWORK-FOR-SEA-2025-2028.pdf), the raw marks are scaled and then weighted before they are combined:

- **Mathematics** carries the full weight of its paper.
- **ELA** (grammar and comprehension) is weighted at roughly 60%.
- **Creative Writing** is weighted at roughly 40%.

The weighted marks are added together into a single number: the **composite score**. That composite — not any individual subject grade — is what the placement system uses.

The practical takeaway is uncomfortable but important: Mathematics carries the heaviest single weight. A child who is strong in writing but shaky in maths can end up with a lower composite than a parent expects. This is not an argument to neglect writing — every mark matters when schools are separated by fractions of a point — but it is an argument to make sure maths is never the quiet weakness nobody is tracking.

## From composite score to a school

Placement is not a fixed pass mark. It is a matching process. Each child submits a list of school choices. The system then works down from the highest composite scores, placing children into their preferred schools until the places at each school run out. The lowest composite score that still secured a place at a given school becomes that school's **cut-off score** for the year.

This has two consequences parents often miss:

1. **Cut-offs move every year.** They depend on how everyone else performed and how many children chose each school. A school's cut-off in 2024 tells you roughly where it sits, not exactly what your child will need. Community-run trackers like [SEAscores.NET](https://seascores.net/blog) publish historical cut-offs so you can see the range.
2. **Your choices matter as much as your marks.** A well-considered list of school choices — a mix of ambitious, realistic, and safe options — can be the difference between a placement your family is happy with and an appeal.

## Why this shapes how you should prepare

Once you understand that a single weighted number decides everything, two things follow.

First, **balance beats brilliance in one subject.** Because the composite adds weighted parts together, the highest-return study time is usually spent shoring up the weakest weighted area, not polishing an already-strong one. A child sitting at 90% in comprehension and 55% in maths will lift their composite far more by moving maths to 70% than by pushing comprehension to 95%.

Second, **you need to actually know where your child stands** in each of the three areas — separately, and honestly. This is where most families are flying blind. A weekly lesson gives a single overall impression. A school term gives a report card months apart. Neither tells you, this week, that fractions are solid but ratio is collapsing. ## The stress nobody weights

There is a fourth factor the framework cannot measure: how your child copes with a single high-stakes morning. The SEA is one exam, on one day, and for an eleven-year-old that pressure is real. UK-style research on high-stakes testing — echoed in local commentary on the [SEA as a high-stakes test](https://www.ukessays.com/essays/education/high-stakes-test-in-trinidad-and-tobago-education-essay.php) — consistently finds that exam anxiety can cost a child marks they would otherwise have earned. Preparation that only drills content, and never rehearses staying calm under timed conditions, leaves marks on the table. We cover this in [SEA Exam Stress: What Helps and What Makes It Worse](/blog/exam-stress-children).

## What to do with this

- Find out, this term, where your child stands **separately** in Maths, ELA and Writing — not just an overall feeling.
- Put the most study time into the weakest **weighted** area, which is often Maths.
- Look up recent cut-offs for the schools you are considering, and build a realistic, layered choice list.
- Rehearse the exam experience, not just the content, so the day itself does not cost marks.

Understanding placement will not raise your child's score by itself. But it stops you from optimising the wrong thing — and in a system decided by fractions of a composite point, spending your child's limited hours on what actually moves the number is the whole game.
