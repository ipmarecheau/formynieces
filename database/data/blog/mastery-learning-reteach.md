There is an old idea in education that keeps proving its worth: do not move a child on to the next topic until they have genuinely mastered the current one. It sounds obvious, yet almost the entire school system is built to violate it — marching every child through a fixed syllabus on a fixed timetable regardless of who has actually understood what. Mastery learning is the deliberate refusal to do that. Understanding why it works, and why re-teaching beats moving on, reshapes how you approach SEA preparation.

## The compounding problem

Learning, especially in maths, compounds. Fractions build on division; ratio builds on fractions; multi-step word problems build on all of it. This means an un-mastered foundation does not just leave one gap — it *undermines everything built on top of it.* A child who never truly grasped fractions will struggle with ratio, percentages and problem-solving, and will look "bad at maths" when in fact they are bad at *one earlier thing* that was never fixed.

The fixed-pace syllabus guarantees this happens. It moves on when the *calendar* says to, not when the *child* is ready, so children accumulate un-mastered foundations and fall progressively further behind, as we describe in [Why One-Size-Fits-All Lessons Leave Children Behind](/blog/one-size-fits-all-lessons). Every topic taught on a shaky base is a topic half-learned.

## What mastery learning does instead

Mastery learning breaks this cycle with a simple rule: **a child advances only when they have demonstrated they understand the current topic** — typically by performing well on fresh questions, not just repeating drilled ones. If they have not, the response is not to move on and hope, nor to simply repeat the same lesson louder. It is to **re-teach** — to diagnose the specific misunderstanding and address *that*, then check again.

This does two things. It ensures every new topic is built on solid ground. And it prevents the demoralising experience of sitting in lessons that assume knowledge the child does not have.

## Re-teaching is not repeating

A crucial distinction: re-teaching is not saying the same thing again. A child who did not understand an explanation the first time usually will not understand the *identical* explanation the second time. Effective re-teaching diagnoses *why* it did not land — which specific step confused them — and comes at it differently: a new example, a different representation, a focus on the exact point of confusion. This is precisely where a responsive, [adaptive](/blog/adaptive-learning-explained) approach shines, because it can tailor the re-teach to the individual child's specific error rather than replaying a generic lesson. We go deeper in [When AI Re-Teaching Genuinely Helps](/blog/ai-reteach-good).

## The evidence, honestly

Mastery learning has a long research pedigree — the idea traces back to Benjamin Bloom's work in the 1960s and 70s, which found that students taught to mastery substantially outperformed those taught conventionally. More recently, intensive mastery-based models have made striking claims. Alpha School's [2-Hour Learning approach](/blog/two-hour-learning-model), for instance, requires students to reach around 90% accuracy before advancing, and reports that its students score in the top percentiles on nationally-normed [MAP tests](https://alpha.school/the-program/).

Honesty requires a caveat: several of these headline results come from the schools' own analyses and have not all been independently verified, and critics rightly note this. So treat the boldest numbers with appropriate caution. But the *underlying principle* — that securing each foundation before building on it produces better learning than marching through regardless — is one of the more robust findings in education, and it does not depend on any single school's marketing.

## Why it matters especially for the SEA

The SEA rewards genuine understanding and punishes shaky foundations, because it tests unfamiliar, multi-step questions that a half-learned topic cannot handle — the exact failure mode of [rote learning](/blog/rote-learning-sea). A mastery approach is therefore not just pedagogically nice; it is strategically correct for this exam. Every topic your child genuinely masters is a topic that will hold under exam pressure. Every topic rushed past is a liability waiting to surface on the day.

## Putting it into practice

You do not need a special school to apply mastery thinking at home:

- **Check for real understanding** before moving on — can the child explain it and solve a *fresh* version?
- **When they can't, re-teach differently** — find the specific confusion, come at it a new way. Don't just repeat.
- **Revisit mastered topics** occasionally to confirm they've stuck, since learning that vanishes was never mastered.
- **Resist the calendar.** Better to deeply master fewer topics than to superficially cover many.

## What to do with this

- Understand that learning **compounds** — an un-mastered foundation undermines everything above it.
- Advance only on demonstrated **mastery**, tested on fresh questions.
- **Re-teach** the specific gap differently; do not just repeat the lesson.
- Treat bold mastery-school statistics with **healthy caution**, but trust the underlying principle.

Moving on feels like progress — a topic ticked, the syllabus advancing. Mastery learning trades that comforting illusion for the real thing: foundations that actually hold. For an exam built to test the unfamiliar, that is the difference that counts.
