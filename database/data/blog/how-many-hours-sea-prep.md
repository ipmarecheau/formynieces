It is one of the most anxious questions a SEA parent asks: *are we doing enough?* And in an extra-lessons culture where some children attend classes after school, on Saturdays, and during holidays, "enough" can quietly inflate until an eleven-year-old is doing a working adult's hours. The honest answer is that the number of hours matters far less than what happens inside them — and beyond a point, more hours actively *hurt*.

## The myth of maximum hours

There is a widespread belief that SEA success is a straight line: more study equals higher marks. If that were true, the child doing six hours a day would always beat the child doing one. Anyone who has watched real children knows it does not work that way. Past a certain point, extra hours produce diminishing returns, then negative ones — a tired, resentful child making careless errors and losing any love of learning.

The reason is that learning is not measured in time served; it is measured in *understanding gained*. An hour of focused, well-targeted practice on a genuine gap beats three hours of unfocused drilling on material already mastered.

## What actually drives progress

Three things determine how much a child learns in a study session, and none of them is raw duration:

**Focus.** Twenty-five fully-attentive minutes on one topic beats ninety distracted minutes. Short, focused sessions with real attention are the workhorse of effective learning. This is partly why some intensive models compress core academics into a couple of focused hours — the [2-Hour Learning approach](/blog/two-hour-learning-model) rests on the claim that highly focused, personalised practice makes long school days unnecessary. Whatever you make of the headline, the underlying principle — focus over duration — is sound.

**Targeting.** An hour spent on the child's *actual* weak spot is worth many hours spent on whatever the worksheet happened to cover. This is why knowing where your child genuinely stands matters more than the timetable — you cannot target a gap you cannot see.

**Mastery, not exposure.** Time spent re-covering material the child already knows feels productive but teaches nothing. Time spent bringing a shaky topic to genuine [mastery](/blog/mastery-learning-reteach) is where progress lives. The goal is understanding, not attendance.

## Why over-drilling backfires

Piling on hours does not just fail to help — it can do real damage. A child drilled to exhaustion arrives at the exam depleted and anxious, and frequently performs *worse* than a well-rested peer who prepared more modestly. Burnout shows up as tears at the desk, physical complaints, a child who once liked a subject now dreading it, and a creeping loss of confidence. These are not signs to push harder; they are signs to pull back. We go deeper in [SEA Exam Stress](/blog/exam-stress-children).

There is also a longer cost. A child taught that learning means grinding misery may pass the SEA and then disengage entirely from school. The SEA is a starting line for seven years of secondary education; winning it at the cost of a child's relationship with learning is a poor trade.

## A sane rhythm

For most children in the SEA years, a sustainable and effective rhythm looks like:

- **A focused daily core** — on the order of an hour to ninety minutes of genuinely attentive academic work, split across subjects, most days.
- **A daily reading habit** — twenty minutes, which does double duty for [ELA](/blog/sea-ela-comprehension) and is restful rather than draining.
- **Weekends lighter, not heavier** — some consolidation, plenty of rest and play. A rested brain consolidates learning; an exhausted one does not.

The exact figure will vary with the child and how close the exam is. But if your child is doing dramatically more than this and still not progressing, the answer is almost never *more hours* — it is *better-targeted* hours.

## The real bottleneck is targeting, not time

Here is the point most timetable-focused parents miss. The reason so many hours get poured in is that nobody can see precisely what to work on, so the strategy defaults to "do more of everything." If you could see, this week, exactly which topics were weak, you could achieve more in a focused hour than in an unfocused three — and hand your child back their evenings. That visibility is the real lever, and its absence is the [tracking gap](/blog/tracking-gap-learning) at work.

## What to do with this

- Stop asking "are we doing enough hours?" and start asking "are these hours **focused and targeted**?"
- Protect a focused daily core plus a daily reading habit; keep weekends lighter.
- Treat burnout signs as a stop signal, not a weakness.
- Invest in *knowing the gaps* so each hour lands where it matters.

More hours is the crudest possible lever, and past a point it pushes the wrong way. Focused, targeted, mastery-driven practice — and a child who is still rested enough to think clearly on exam morning — beats sheer volume every time.
