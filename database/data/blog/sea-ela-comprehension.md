English Language Arts carries around 60% of the ELA weighting in the SEA composite — the grammar and comprehension paper alongside the separate [Creative Writing](/blog/sea-creative-writing-tips) component. Many families treat ELA as the "easy" paper compared with maths, and pay it less attention. That is a mistake. Comprehension in particular is where careful children win marks that careless ones throw away, and it rewards a skill — genuine reading — that cannot be crammed in the final weeks.

## Comprehension is a reading skill, not a guessing game

The comprehension section gives a passage and asks questions about it. It sounds simple, but children lose marks in predictable ways: they answer from memory or assumption instead of from the text, they miss the exact word the question turns on, or they give a vague answer where a precise one was required.

The habits that fix this are teachable:

- **Read the passage first, then the questions, then the passage again** with the questions in mind. The second read is targeted.
- **Underline the evidence.** Every answer to a "what/why/how" question should be traceable to a specific place in the text. If a child cannot point to where the answer lives, they are guessing.
- **Watch the question word.** "List" wants items; "explain" wants reasons; "in your own words" penalises copying. Many marks are lost simply by not doing what the question asked.
- **Answer in full.** A one-word answer to a two-mark question usually earns one mark. Teaching a child to give the *complete* answer is pure, free marks.

## Grammar: the rules that actually recur

The grammar portion tests the mechanics of English — parts of speech, tense, subject-verb agreement, punctuation, sentence types. These follow rules, and the same rules recur every year. That makes grammar genuinely learnable to a high standard, unlike the open-ended writing paper.

The trap is treating grammar as isolated worksheet drills disconnected from real language. A child who can label a noun on a worksheet but writes sentence fragments in their composition has learned the *test* of grammar, not grammar. The fix is to connect the two: when you correct a tense error in your child's writing, name the rule; when you drill a rule, point to it in something they are reading. Grammar and writing reinforce each other.

## Why wide reading is the real engine

Here is the quiet truth about ELA: the children who score highest are almost always the ones who *read widely for pleasure*. Reading builds vocabulary in context (far stickier than word lists), an ear for correct grammar (so errors "sound wrong"), and the stamina to work through a dense passage without losing the thread. None of that can be manufactured in a revision sprint — it is built over years, twenty minutes at a time.

If you do one thing for your child's ELA, make it a daily reading habit. A short reading-and-vocabulary ritual — a passage, a few new words met in context, a couple of questions — compounds quietly over the SEA years into exactly the skills the paper rewards. It is the least glamorous and most effective ELA preparation there is.

## Don't neglect ELA because it "feels fine"

Because a child can *speak* English fluently, parents often assume the ELA paper will look after itself. But conversational fluency and exam comprehension are different skills. A child can be perfectly articulate and still lose marks by not underlining evidence, misreading question words, or giving incomplete answers. The only way to know is to see how they perform on *fresh* comprehension passages — not how they chat at the dinner table.

This is the same visibility problem that haunts every SEA subject: without seeing your child work through real, unseen material, you cannot tell confident-sounding from actually-solid. We cover this gap in [Measuring SEA Progress](/blog/measuring-progress-sea).

## A weekly ELA rhythm

- **Daily reading** — twenty minutes of something the child enjoys, with a few new words discussed.
- **Twice-weekly comprehension** — one fresh passage, worked with the underlining habit, answers marked for completeness.
- **Short, connected grammar** — a rule at a time, linked back to the child's own writing.

Keep it light and consistent rather than heavy and occasional. ELA rewards the slow accumulation of a reading mind, not last-minute cramming.

## What to do with this

- Treat comprehension as a **reading-with-evidence** skill, not a guessing game.
- Teach children to watch the question word and answer in full.
- Learn grammar rules, but connect them to real reading and writing.
- Above all, build a **daily reading habit** — it is the engine of ELA.
- Check performance on *fresh* passages, not on how fluent your child sounds.

ELA is not the easy paper. It is the paper that rewards a genuine reader — and the good news is that the habit that wins it, daily reading, is also one of the best gifts you can give a child for life.
