Most families measure SEA progress with a single instrument: the weekly test score. The child sits something, a number comes out, and that number becomes the family's read on how things are going. It is better than nothing — but a lone score is a noisy, shallow measure that can mislead as often as it informs. If you want to actually steer your child's preparation, you need to measure the right things, in the right way.

## Why a single score misleads

A weekly score bounces around for reasons that have nothing to do with real learning: which topics happened to be on it, how the child slept, whether the questions echoed ones recently drilled. A child can score 80% one week and 60% the next while their underlying understanding barely changed — the difference was the mix of questions. Reading trend into that noise leads to false alarms and false comfort in equal measure.

Worse, a single percentage **averages away the detail you need**. "68%" tells you nothing about *which third* your child got wrong, or *why*. Was it a content gap, a misreading, a careless slip, or running out of time? Those demand completely different responses, as we cover in [Using SEA Past Papers the Right Way](/blog/sea-past-papers). The score hides all of it.

## The three things worth measuring

Genuine progress shows up in three dimensions a raw score cannot capture.

**Mastery, topic by topic.** The useful question is not "what did they score?" but "which specific topics has my child genuinely mastered, and which are shaky?" A topic-level map — fractions: solid; ratio: shaky; measurement: not started — is worth a dozen aggregate scores, because it tells you exactly what to do next. This is the visibility most parents lack, as we argue in [Why Parents Can't See What Their Child Is Learning](/blog/parent-visibility-learning).

**Retention over time.** Learning that vanishes in a fortnight was never learned. Real progress means a topic mastered in March is *still* solid in May. Measuring retention requires topics to reappear later — a child who never revisits fractions may have quietly lost them while the weekly scores looked fine. Progress is what *stays*.

**Performance on unseen questions.** The SEA tests unfamiliar material. So the truest measure of progress is how a child handles questions they have *not* practised — fresh phrasings, combined topics, novel contexts. A rising score on re-used questions can be pure memory, an illusion of progress we describe in [rote learning](/blog/rote-learning-sea). Performance on genuinely fresh questions cannot be faked.

## From measurement to action

Measurement only matters if it changes what you do. The point of a topic-level mastery map that tracks retention and unseen performance is that it *tells you where to put the next hour*. Ratio shaky and not improving? That is the session. Fractions mastered and holding? Leave them, apart from occasional review. This is how you escape the trap of pouring in undifferentiated [hours](/blog/how-many-hours-sea-prep) — you finally know where to aim.

A good measurement system answers, at any moment: *What has my child mastered? What is slipping? What should we work on next?* Those three questions are the whole job.

## Why families rarely have this

If topic-level, retention-aware, unseen-question measurement is so much better, why does almost no one have it? Because assembling it by hand is genuinely hard. It means recording every practice attempt by topic, noting *why* each error happened, bringing topics back to test retention, and keeping it all in one running picture rather than scattered across marked papers and memory. No busy parent, and no tutor with a full class, can sustain that manually. The information exists but stays fragmented — the [tracking gap](/blog/tracking-gap-learning) again.

This is precisely the kind of bookkeeping software is good at: quietly recording every attempt, sorting by topic, resurfacing old material to check retention, and showing the parent one honest, current map. Not to replace judgement, but to give judgement something real to work with.

## What to do with this

- Stop reading a single weekly score as "progress" — it is noisy and shallow.
- Measure **mastery by topic**, **retention over time**, and **performance on unseen questions**.
- Use the measurement to decide **where the next session goes**.
- Recognise that keeping this picture by hand is nearly impossible — which is exactly why it is usually missing.

A number at the top of a test is a snapshot in poor light. What you actually want is a clear, current, honest map of your child's learning — what is solid, what is slipping, and what to do next. Measure that, and every hour of preparation gets sharper.
