The word "journal" sounds passive — a diary, a record of things that happened. But a learning journal can be something far more powerful: the mechanism that closes the *feedback loop* between what a child is taught, what they are tested on, and what they practise next. When a journal captures a child's real school work and feeds it back into their ongoing learning, it stops being a record and becomes an engine. Here is how that works, and why it matters for the SEA.

## What a feedback loop is

In any system that improves, a feedback loop is the cycle: *act, measure the result, adjust, act again.* A thermostat measures the temperature and adjusts the heat. A good athlete watches footage of their performance and changes their training. Learning works the same way — a child should practise, find out what they got wrong and why, and have their next practice adjust accordingly.

The trouble is that in most children's learning, the loop is **broken**. The child does school work, it gets marked, and the marked work goes into a bag, a drawer, or the bin. The single richest source of information about what the child actually knows — their real, teacher-assessed work — never feeds back into what they practise next. The loop is open; the signal is lost.

## The school journal as the missing link

This is where a school journal earns its name. Instead of letting marked school work vanish, the journal captures it — the class test, the teacher's comments, the score, the specific questions missed. And crucially, it does not just *store* that work; it *reads* it and feeds it back into the child's learning picture.

That single move closes the loop. The topic the child got wrong on Friday's class test becomes the topic their practice targets over the weekend. The teacher's assessment — normally a dead end — becomes a live input. The school channel, which we described as the biggest disconnected silo in [School, Lessons, Apps](/blog/multiple-learning-channels), finally connects to home practice.

## Why the school's own work is the best signal

You might ask: why bother capturing school work when a child can just do practice questions at home? Because the school's marked work carries signal that home practice cannot replicate:

- It is **assessed by the teacher** who knows the child and the curriculum.
- It happens under **real classroom conditions**, including the timed pressure that home practice often lacks.
- It reflects **what the school is actually emphasising** right now.

A gap that shows up on a real school test — not just a home app — is a gap you can trust. Feeding that trusted signal back into practice is far more valuable than practising blind. It also lets you compare: does the child's confident home performance hold up on the school's timed test? That comparison, only possible when both live in one place, is exactly the cross-channel [measurability](/blog/consolidating-learning-data) that catches problems early.

## From record to action

The difference between a passive record and a feedback loop is *what happens next*. A journal that merely files marked work is a tidy archive. A journal that closes the loop does three things with each entry:

1. **Reads** the work — which topics, which questions, what the child got wrong.
2. **Updates** the child's honest, topic-level picture of what is mastered and what is slipping — the [visibility](/blog/parent-visibility-learning) parents so rarely have.
3. **Adjusts** what the child practises next, so the next session targets the real gap the school work revealed.

That is a loop: school work in, updated picture, targeted practice, better school work, and around again. Each turn of the loop makes the next practice sharper.

## An honest note on what it can and cannot do

Capturing and reading school work is genuinely useful, but it is not infallible. A photographed test has to be read correctly; a score has to be interpreted in context; a single bad test day is not proof of a lasting gap. A good journal treats each entry as *one signal among several*, to be reconciled with the child's practice history rather than trusted blindly — and it flags uncertainty rather than pretending to certainty it does not have. The goal is a truer picture over time, not a false precision from any single photo.

Used this way, a learning journal turns the most under-used resource in a child's education — their own marked school work — into the thing that steers what they do next. That is the difference between a diary and a feedback loop.

## What to do with this

- Stop letting marked school work **vanish** — it is the richest signal you have.
- Treat the journal as a **loop**, not an archive: read the work, update the picture, adjust practice.
- Trust the school's **assessed, timed** work as an especially reliable gap signal.
- Expect the journal to handle each entry as **one signal among several**, honestly, not as gospel.

Close the loop, and every class test your child sits stops being a dead end and starts making their next week of learning smarter.
