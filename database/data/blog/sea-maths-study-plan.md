Mathematics carries the single heaviest weight in the SEA composite score, which makes it the highest-return subject to get right — and the most damaging to get wrong. Yet maths is also the subject most often "prepared" by sheer volume: stacks of past papers, endless worksheets, a child grinding through problems they half-understand. There is a better way, and it is built on one idea: **mastery before movement.**

## Why volume alone fails

A child who does fifty questions on fractions but never understood *why* the method works has not learned fractions — they have memorised a procedure. Under the pressure of a scaled, standardised exam that deliberately includes unfamiliar phrasings, memorised procedures collapse. The child freezes on a question that looks different from the ones they drilled, even though the underlying maths is identical.

This is the core weakness of [rote learning](/blog/rote-learning-sea): it produces fluency on the *seen* and helplessness on the *unseen*. The SEA is designed to test the unseen.

## The mastery loop

Instead of "do more questions", structure maths study as a loop, one topic at a time:

1. **Teach the concept** — genuinely, until the child can explain it back in their own words.
2. **Practise deliberately** — a modest set of questions that vary in surface form.
3. **Check for mastery** — can they get, say, 80%+ on *fresh* questions?
4. **If not, re-teach** the specific misunderstanding — not the whole topic again.
5. **Only then move on.**

This is the principle behind [mastery learning](/blog/mastery-learning-reteach), and it is exactly why some intensive models — like the [2-Hour Learning approach](/blog/two-hour-learning-model) used by Alpha School — insist on around 90% accuracy before a child advances. The point is not perfectionism; it is that moving on from a shaky foundation guarantees the next topic will be shaky too.

## A realistic weekly rhythm

You do not need three hours a night. For most children in the SEA years, a sustainable maths rhythm looks like:

- **20–35 focused minutes, most days.** Short, frequent, and fully attentive beats long, exhausted sessions. We discuss why in [How Many Hours of SEA Prep](/blog/how-many-hours-sea-prep).
- **One topic in focus at a time**, until it reaches mastery, rather than skipping across the syllabus.
- **A weekly "mixed" session** where older topics reappear, so mastered material is not quietly forgotten — the "use it or lose it" effect is real in maths.

## Sequence the topics deliberately

SEA maths builds on itself. Number sense and the four operations underpin fractions; fractions underpin decimals, percentages and ratio; all of it underpins the multi-step word problems that carry the heaviest marks. If ratio is collapsing, the cause is often an un-mastered fraction concept two steps back.

This is why chasing "hard" topics first is usually a mistake. A child who cannot fluently handle fractions will keep hitting a wall on everything downstream. Fix the foundation, and the harder topics often become manageable without extra drilling.

## Word problems are a reading skill too

A large share of SEA maths marks live in worded, multi-step problems. Many children lose these marks not on the maths but on the *comprehension* — they misread what is being asked. Practising the habit of underlining what is given, circling what is asked, and translating words into a number sentence is as valuable as the arithmetic itself. It also quietly reinforces the ELA comprehension skills covered in [Cracking SEA ELA](/blog/sea-ela-comprehension).

## Protect against burnout

The child who is drilled to exhaustion arrives at the exam depleted, anxious, and often *worse* than a well-rested peer who prepared more modestly. Burnout is not a sign of commitment; it is a preparation failure. Watch for the warning signs — tears at the desk, physical complaints, a child who used to like maths now dreading it — and treat them as data, not defiance. A calm, confident child on exam morning outperforms a burnt-out one, as we explain in [SEA Exam Stress](/blog/exam-stress-children).

## Know where the gaps actually are

Every strategy here assumes you know *which* maths topics are solid and which are collapsing — this week, not last term. Most parents do not have that visibility. A weekly lesson gives an overall impression; it rarely tells you that place value is fine but long division just fell apart. Without that resolution, you cannot target the next 30 minutes where they will help most, and you risk re-drilling what is already mastered while the real gap grows. This is the [tracking gap](/blog/tracking-gap-learning) in miniature.

## What to do with this

- Prioritise maths — it carries the most weight in the composite.
- Work in a **mastery loop**: teach, practise, check, re-teach, then move on.
- Keep sessions short, frequent and focused; protect against burnout.
- Fix foundations before chasing "hard" topics.
- Insist on knowing which specific topics are weak, so every session targets the real gap.

Good SEA maths preparation is not about doing the most questions. It is about making sure every topic is genuinely understood before the next one is built on top of it — and knowing, honestly, where your child stands so no session is wasted.
