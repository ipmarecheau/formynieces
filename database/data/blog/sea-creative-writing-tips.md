Creative Writing is weighted at around 40% of the ELA component of the SEA, which makes it far too important to leave to chance — yet it is the paper most often "prepared" by memorising a handful of set essays and hoping one fits the prompt. Markers see straight through this. The children who genuinely score well are not the ones with the most pre-written stories; they are the ones who can structure a response, reach for precise vocabulary, and write with a voice of their own. All three are teachable.

## What markers are actually looking for

SEA writing is assessed on a combination of ideas, organisation, language, and mechanics. In plain terms, a strong piece:

- **Answers the prompt directly** — it does not bolt a memorised story onto a question it does not fit.
- **Has a clear shape** — a beginning that sets the scene, a middle with a genuine problem or development, and an ending that resolves rather than trails off.
- **Uses precise, varied vocabulary** — not the longest words a child can find, but the *right* words used correctly.
- **Controls the basics** — spelling, punctuation, sentence boundaries, tense.

The child who memorises one "perfect" essay tends to lose marks on the first point and the third: the piece does not truly fit the prompt, and the vocabulary is impressive-but-misused. Markers reward *fit* and *control* over showy padding.

## Why memorised essays backfire

The instinct to bank a ready-made composition is understandable — it feels safe. But it fails for the same reason [rote learning fails in maths](/blog/rote-learning-sea): it produces performance on the expected and collapse on the unexpected. When the prompt does not match the memorised piece, the child either forces an awkward fit (obvious to markers) or panics. Worse, memorised writing has no *voice* — and voice is exactly what lifts a competent piece into a memorable one.

## Building the skill instead

Real improvement comes from a small number of habits, practised regularly:

**Plan before writing.** Two minutes spent jotting a beginning-middle-end and one strong idea prevents the rambling, shapeless piece that loses organisation marks. Children resist planning because it feels slow; show them it is the fastest route to a coherent story.

**Grow vocabulary in context, not lists.** A word learned from a list is forgotten by exam day. A word met in reading, discussed, and then *used* in the child's own writing sticks. This is one reason wide reading is the quiet secret of strong writers — and why it also lifts the [comprehension paper](/blog/sea-ela-comprehension).

**Write regularly, and get specific feedback.** A child who writes one composition a week and receives precise feedback — "this paragraph has three ideas competing; pick one" — improves far faster than one who writes ten with only a mark at the top. Vague feedback ("good, add more detail") does almost nothing. The feedback has to point at the exact thing to change.

**Read your child's writing aloud together.** Reading a piece aloud exposes clumsy sentences, missing full stops, and repetition far better than silent re-reading. It also builds the child's own ear for what good writing sounds like.

## The feedback problem

Notice that every method above depends on **specific, timely feedback** — and that is exactly what is hardest for a busy parent or a crowded lesson to provide consistently. One tutor with fifteen children cannot give each composition the line-by-line attention that actually moves the needle. This is where honest, targeted feedback tools earn their place: not to write *for* the child, but to point precisely at what to fix, every time, so the child does the improving. We explore the good and bad versions of this in [Can AI Really Personalise a Child's Learning?](/blog/ai-personalised-learning).

A warning, though: feedback that only ever flatters is worse than useless. A tool — or a person — that praises every piece equally teaches a child nothing about where they actually stand. The evidence on why [AI that flatters is dangerous](/blog/ai-flattery-learning) applies directly here.

## Rehearse under real conditions

The SEA writing paper is timed, and time pressure is where planning and control break down. A child who only ever writes at leisure will over-run or freeze on the day. Practising the full cycle — read prompt, plan, write, check — inside the real time limit turns a good writer into a good *exam* writer. The same principle applies across the whole exam, as we discuss in [SEA Exam Stress](/blog/exam-stress-children).

## What to do with this

- Teach your child to **plan** every piece, however briefly.
- Grow vocabulary through reading and use, not memorised lists.
- Insist on **specific** feedback that names the one thing to fix.
- Ban memorised essays; rehearse fitting a response to *any* prompt.
- Practise under the real time limit before exam day.

Creative Writing rewards children who can think and shape their own ideas under pressure — not those with the best-rehearsed script. Build the skill, give precise feedback, and let your child's own voice come through.
