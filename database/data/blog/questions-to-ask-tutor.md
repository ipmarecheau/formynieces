Choosing extra lessons or a tutor is one of the bigger investments a SEA family makes — in money, in time, and in a child's limited energy. Yet most families choose largely on reputation and convenience, and rarely ask the questions that reveal whether a given lesson will actually *teach their specific child*. Here is a practical checklist. The answers, and how readily they come, tell you a great deal.

## On diagnosis

**"How will you find out what my child specifically struggles with?"**

This is the most important question, and the answers vary enormously. A strong provider has a real method — diagnostic work, regular checks on individual understanding, attention to each child's errors. A weak one will talk only about "covering the syllabus." As we explain in [The Hidden Problem With Extra Lessons](/blog/problem-with-extra-lessons), a tutor who cannot diagnose your individual child cannot target their teaching, no matter how good the general instruction is.

**"How big is the group?"**

Pace and diagnosis both depend on it. A tutor with six children can track each; one with twenty-five is delivering a lecture. This is the [one-size-fits-all](/blog/one-size-fits-all-lessons) problem in miniature — the larger the group, the more your child is served only if they happen to sit at the class's chosen pace.

## On response

**"What happens when my child hasn't mastered something before the class moves on?"**

Listen carefully here. Does the lesson march through the syllabus on a fixed timetable regardless, or is there a mechanism to catch and rebuild a shaky foundation? A child carried forward on an un-mastered base falls further behind every week, as we describe in [rote learning](/blog/rote-learning-sea) and [mastery learning](/blog/mastery-learning-reteach). "We move at the pace of the syllabus" is an honest answer — and a warning.

**"What kind of feedback does my child get?"**

A score at the top of a page is not feedback. Useful feedback names the *specific* thing to fix — "you're losing comprehension marks by not answering in full," not "do better." Ask for an example of the feedback a child actually receives. Vague or purely encouraging feedback teaches little, a point that applies to humans and software alike.

## On honesty

**"Will you tell me if my child is falling behind — clearly?"**

Some providers, understandably wanting to keep customers happy, soften bad news until it is nearly useless. You need a provider who will tell you the truth, even when it is uncomfortable, because you cannot fix a problem you are not told about. Beware the lesson that only ever reports sunshine — the same danger as [AI that flatters](/blog/ai-flattery-learning), where pleasant feedback that ignores real weaknesses actively harms the child.

**"What visibility will *I* get into how my child is doing?"**

Will you receive specific, current information about which topics are solid and which are weak — or just a general "she's doing well"? As we argue in [Why Parents Can't See What Their Child Is Learning](/blog/parent-visibility-learning), you are entitled to real visibility into something you are paying for. A provider who offers a genuine window into your child's progress is worth more than one who offers only reassurance.

## On fit and approach

**"How do you handle a child who is ahead on some topics and behind on others?"**

Every child is uneven across topics. A thoughtful provider has an answer for this — some form of differentiation. A provider who treats the class as a single unit does not, which means your child is well served only on the topics where they happen to match the group.

**"Do you build understanding, or teach methods to memorise?"**

The answer to this often comes through in *how* they explain their teaching. Do they talk about children understanding *why*, being able to explain and apply — or about drilling procedures until they stick? The first survives the exam's unfamiliar questions; the second does not.

## Reading the answers

You are not looking for perfect answers — no lesson is flawless, and an honest "we do our best but with a large group there are limits" is more trustworthy than a slick pitch. You are looking for:

- A **real method** for diagnosing your individual child.
- A **mechanism** for responding when your child hasn't mastered something.
- **Specific, honest feedback** — including bad news.
- Genuine **visibility** for you.

If those are present, the lesson is likely to teach *your* child, not just occupy them. If the answers are all vague reassurance, you may be buying attendance, and you will need to supply the diagnosis, targeting and visibility yourself — through home practice and tools built for exactly that.

## What to do with this

- Ask how the provider will **diagnose your specific child**, and how big the group is.
- Ask what happens when your child **hasn't mastered** something on schedule.
- Ask for the **kind of feedback** given, and whether they will tell you bad news plainly.
- Insist on real **visibility**, and favour understanding over memorised methods.

The right questions, asked before you commit, save months of paying for lessons that were never going to reach your particular child. And whatever you choose, pairing it with targeted, visible home practice fills the gaps that any group setting inevitably leaves.
